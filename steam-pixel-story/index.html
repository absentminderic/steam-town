What makes a game popular? Title

NMC5344 group project · November 2026

What makes a game popular?

Steam lists tens of thousands of games, and most of them are barely played. We looked at 89,624 of them to see which design choices go with reaching more players.

Every pixel on this screen is one game.

▼ Scroll to start

Stage 1 · The data

89,624 games, one pixel each

Our file is the Steam Games Dataset 2025 from Kaggle, built from Steam store pages, the Steam API and SteamSpy. It runs up to 10 March 2025.

Each game comes with its price, DLC count, supported systems and languages, and an estimated number of owners. Owners are our measure of popularity.

Stage 2 · Clean-up

8,507 games sit this one out

  • 8,418 games have an owner estimate of “0 – 0”. Steam has no estimate for them, which is not the same as zero players.
  • 79 games list no supported language.
  • 10 rows have broken values, such as text where a number should be.

That leaves 81,117 games for the charts.

Stage 3 · Loot tiers

Only 9.4% of games are Rare or better

Owner estimates come in ranges, so we sorted the games into five loot tiers. Rare or better means 100,000 owners or more, and those games sit on the left of the block.

Stage 4 · Power-up 1

Run on more systems

Of games on a single operating system (almost always Windows), 7.8% reach 100,000 owners. Add a second system and it is 12.2%.

Games on Windows, macOS and Linux reach 17.8%, more than twice the single-system rate.

Stage 5 · Power-up 2

Speak more languages

The share reaching 100,000 owners climbs from 5.2% for one language to 7.5% for two to five, 20.1% for six to ten and 24.3% for 11 or more.

The big jump comes at six languages.

Stage 6 · Power-up 3

Add DLC

26.8% of games with DLC reach 100,000 owners, against 6.0% of games without. That is about four and a half times the rate.

Cause and effect can run both ways here: studios tend to make DLC for games that already sell.

Stage 7 · Combo

Stack the power-ups

Take games on one system with no DLC. Of those in one language, 3.5% reach 100,000 owners. Of those in 11 or more languages, 11.1% do.

Each waffle has 100 squares, and each square is 1% of the games in that group.

Stage 7 · Combo x3

Now add six or more DLC

The same two groups climb to 22.2% and 78.2%. Wide language support and lots of DLC together go with by far the widest reach.

Stage 9 · Boss fight

The model fights back

Next we fitted four regression models that predict owners from systems, languages and DLC. The best one explained just 5.5% of the differences between games.

It also said each extra language went with fewer owners, the opposite of what Stage 5 shows.

Stage 9 · Plot twist

The culprit: games with no estimate

The games with a “0 – 0” estimate were still in the models, counted as zero owners. They list 13 languages on average, against 4 for every other game, and 63% of the games that claim more than 50 languages have no estimate.

Take them out, as the charts already did, and more languages go with more owners. The best model now explains 15.4%.

Stage 10 · Results

What the pixels say

  • Being easy to reach goes with reaching more players: more systems, more languages.
  • DLC goes with reach too, though popular games also tend to get more DLC.
  • Together these features explain about 15% of the differences in owners. The rest comes down to everything else: genre, quality, price, marketing and luck.

Patch notes

Known issues with this data.

  • EstimatesOwners are estimated ranges, not exact counts. The tiers use the lower end of each range.
  • Steam only“I played AION 2 on the Taiwan server before it ever reached Steam. Steam never counted me.” (@absentminderic)
  • Cut-offThe data stops on 10 March 2025. inZOI launched on 28 March, 18 days too late to make the file.
  • CausalityEverything here is an association. None of these charts can say what causes what.
  • Model checkStage 9 re-runs our four models with and without the “0 – 0” games. Both versions are in the tables below.
